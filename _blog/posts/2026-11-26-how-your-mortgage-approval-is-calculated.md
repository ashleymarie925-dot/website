Welcome to the first post in my new series, **What People Don't Know About Buying a House**! Each one covers something most buyers never hear until they're already in the middle of the process. Knowing it early can change what you qualify for.

Let's start with the big one: how your approval amount is actually calculated.

## We don't care how much you owe

I know, that sounds backwards! But when we figure out how much house you can buy, we don't use your overall debt. We only use your **monthly** debt.

Owing $30,000 on a car doesn't matter to your approval. The $500 monthly payment does.

## The simple math

1. We start with your monthly income before taxes.
2. We subtract your monthly debt payments (car, credit card minimums, student loans, and so on).
3. What's left is what we use to calculate your approval amount.

This is called your **debt-to-income ratio**. So if you'd like a higher approval amount, there are two ways to get there: add income, or lower the monthly debt we have to count.

## Way 1: Add income with a co-signer

A co-signer's income can help balance out your monthly debt. Ideally it's someone who will live in the house with you.

If your co-signer won't be living there, we have to count their own rent or mortgage in the debt calculation too, which takes away some of the help.

## Way 2: Lower the monthly debt we count

At today's rates, every $50 a month in debt payments is worth roughly $7,000 in loan amount. That adds up fast!

And here's the fun part: there are a few ways a debt can stop counting **without being fully paid off**.

### The 10 payment rule

A fun fact that not a lot of people know: you don't actually have to pay all of a debt off for us to not count it. On many conventional loans, if an installment loan (like a car loan) has 10 or fewer payments left, we may not have to count it at all.

Here's an example:

- You have a $20,000 loan with a $500 monthly payment.
- You pay it down to $5,000 or less. That's 10 payments or fewer remaining.
- That $500 a month can come out of the math.

Taking a $500 payment out of the picture is worth roughly $70,000 more in buying power, without paying off the full $20,000.

### Student loans

This is my favorite example. It doesn't matter if you have $200,000 in student loans. What matters is your monthly payment. And some people don't even have one, so on certain loan programs we don't have to count it at all!

Here are the ways that payment gets set:

- **If you're making a regular payment,** we generally use that payment.
- **If your loans are deferred or show a $0 payment,** we use half a percent or one percent of the balance, depending on the loan program we go through. On a $40,000 balance, that's $200 or $400 a month.
- **If you're on an income-driven plan,** like Income-Based Repayment (IBR), your real payment may be much lower. On some conventional loans, a documented $0 IBR payment means we may not have to count your student loans at all!

That's why two buyers with the exact same student loans can qualify for very different amounts.

### Loans you co-signed for someone else

Say a family member or friend had you co-sign on their car. It shows up on your credit, so it looks like your payment. It's possible to leave that debt out of your monthly calculation when:

- They've been making the payments for more than 12 months,
- The payments come out of their own account (one that doesn't have your name on it), and
- The payments have been made on time.

## The takeaway

Your approval amount isn't set in stone. It moves with which debts count, and a lot of people have more room than they think.

The best first step is to look at your own numbers **before** you pay anything off, so every dollar goes where it helps you most. That's exactly what a game plan is for.
